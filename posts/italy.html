Italy: Rome, Florence, Venice

by Evan

Before I talk about Italy, let me first fill you in on how we got from Barcelona to Rome. We took a ferry, overnight, that lasted about twenty hours. Now, maybe you're thinking like I did — it's basically a cruise, so there will be some good food, casino, swimming pool on the deck, topside bar. It'll be a great way to get a taste of the Mediterranean. You might actually look forward to it, like I was.

I should have been tipped off by the fact that only about a hundred people total got on board (the ship probably could hold a couple thousand). Turns out there's a reason the human race has mostly advanced past water travel through the invention and use of trains, airplanes, cars, bicycles, unicycles, vespas, rickshaws, hang gliders, and walking... any which method would have been preferable to the ferry. The one redeeming factor of the trip was that since so few people were traveling in our galley everyone could lie down across 3 seats to catch some sleep. As for the amenities, they were either nonexistent or prohibitively expensive. Blankets and pillows? No. Internet? 5 euros for fifteen minutes. A beer? 7 euros. Swimming pool? No water.

But you know what, it finally landed in Civitavecchia and I was all too happy to board our train to take the hour-long ride into the city.

Rome · June 2–6

It honestly feels like just a few days ago when we arrived at Roma Termini, got ripped off by our cab driver for a few extra euros, and rang the bell of our rented apartment, only to be answered by an Italian lady. For our travels through Italy we were meeting up with Jennifer and three of her friends, and in Rome we rented an apartment (great deal — cheapest place we're staying all trip, best location relative to interesting sights, and most spacious). They arrived early and Jennifer sent us instructions on which doorbell to ring at the front door, and said they'd be waiting. The Italian lady clearly was not Jennifer. So we waited awhile outside the apartment before finally just trying buttons at random. A few tries later and we get Jennifer on the line, nevermind the fact that the doorbells don't match up to their corresponding rooms at all.

This actually set a tone for a lot of things in Italy. I'd heard stories and stereotypes that Italy is a chaotic place, where the only thing you can count on is that nothing will work smoothly or perfectly. We basically found this to be true, starting with the door bell, and continuing during our travels through Rome, Florence, and Venice.

While the Italian repairman was literally just hitting the hot water heater with a hammer to fix it, we decided to get an early start on our first full day in Italy and hit Rome hard with a trip to the Vatican, intending to see St. Peter's Cathedral, the Sistine Chapel, and the Museum.

As we worked our way past the countless shops selling rosary beads, crucifixes, and portraits of Pope John Paul II, the great Cathedral gradually came into focus, and before it the even greater line of people waiting to get in. Panicking, we opted for one of the guided tours, paying a twenty euro price over the ticket to get in. In retrospect, it was actually completely worth it. It's a shame the tour guides seem so sketchy as you walk up to St. Peters. They behaved exactly how you would expect someone soliciting drugs or prostitutes to act, slyly coming up and saying softly, "Hey stud, looking for a good time? (on our tour)"... Or something like that. Anyway, we took the plunge, and after being led about a kilometer away to their tour office near the museum entrance, waiting on tickets, and handing over our monopoly money (aka Euros), we got to skip the line into the museum (which was about equal to the cathedral line... huge).

The Vatican Museum itself is absolutely huge, similar in scale to the Louvre, and we got a whirlwind tour, hitting all the highlights of renown medieval and renaissance art. I'm no art major, so the fast paced highlight tour was really ideal for me. After it was done, the day in the Papal State only got better though. Our tour finished in the Sistine Chapel, which words and pictures really do not do justice — it's one of those things that you really have to see in person to really appreciate its magnitude and complexity. It wasn't even slightly spoiled by the incessant whispers (they pack a lot of people into that room) or the guard whose only job is to shout at people "NO PICTURES!"

Besides the guided tour itself, another perk of spending a few extra euros was the privilege to go immediately from the Sistine Chapel straight to St. Peter's without waiting in another line. The cathedral was another jaw-dropping, stunning experience. It's HUGE. You might think Notre Dame is a big cathedral, sure, but it's nothing in comparison. It's a similar experience to looking down on cars and houses from an airplane and realizing just how small you really are, except instead of looking down you're just looking up and up and up into the rafters.

After being sufficiently awed for one day, we headed out for some late lunch, following the basic formula of "go in the complete opposite direction of tourist sites for several blocks" to find a good place, and it worked. We all got some great pasta, but the highlight was Jennifer's four cheese pizza. Just about every meal we had in Italy was great, but that pizza was absolutely exceptional. In Italy you might be tempted to order some of the fancier pastas to take full advantage of the fine Italian cuisine, but you really don't have to. Often the simpler dishes are really the best ones you can get, and this pizza exemplified that rule.

For our second day in Rome we opted out of a day-trip to Pompeii taken by Jennifer & friends, and instead explored the large complex of Roman ruins consisting of the Forum, Palatine Hill, and the Colosseum. We headed off to the forum, where we came across the first instance of Rick Steves leading us astray. Using his map of Rome, we made our way to one of the marked entrances to the site, but it was completely run down and overgrown. What ensued was us trudging through the winding streets of Rome, doing almost a full circle around the Forum and Palatine Hill, and finally getting to the entrance. One benefit to this roundabout path was finding a tiny farmer's market with one man selling "fresh, uncooked (unpasteurized)" milk. We bought a liter for a euro and it was the best milk I've ever had. You don't really think of milk as being something that has different degrees of taste from vendor to vendor, but the milk we had that day puts the liquid in a whole new light.

Walking around all Roman Ruins you really get a great sense of just how ancient the city of Rome actually is, and how impressive the Romans actually were at construction. The Colosseum is of course massive, and it's a little unsettling to stand in the Curia and think about how 2000 years ago decisions that affected the entire civilized world were made in that same building.

The next morning we went to the Pantheon, another 2000 year old wonder of roman engineering, and a building that still has the largest unsupported concrete dome anywhere in the world, or something like that. When we first arrived it was actually being used for Sunday mass! Thank goodness the Catholics decided to convert the temple into a cathedral, otherwise we probably wouldn't have the privilege of being able to see it standing today.

After the Pantheon it started raining, which unfortunately has been happening a lot during our trip. We weren't going to let that stop us from enjoying our last afternoon in Rome, however, and Hannah and I fearlessly took out our fabulous white umbrella and set off. We saw the Trevi fountain and tossed in our coins (according to legend, ensuring our return to Rome), walked up the Spanish Steps without slipping on the wet marble, and trudged through the wet Villa Borghese to their Gallery, which houses some of the most famous Renaissance sculptures including Bernini's Apollo and Daphne. Unfortunately the Gallery had been sold out, so we dried off a little in the museum gift shop before walking back.

That night we got a table for all six of us at Da Buffetto's pizzeria, much loved by Houston Chronicle writer Ken Hoffman. While the pizza didn't knock everybody over like the four cheese earlier, all of it was great, and it was a fantastic last meal in Rome.

Florence · June 6–8

Get in, wait for Jen and co., then head out. Lines at the Duomo really long, try to head away to a cheaper/better restaurant not on the tourist drag — success! Spot a place with lots of people in suits (businessmen) so we dive in and grab a table for 7. Delicious food, speedy and courteous service.

If you're traveling in groups, be prepared to switch off credit cards. Makes everything MUCH easier — just write down who owes what and figure it out all at once.

Go to tourist market then split off group — can only take so many leather stands selling exactly the same thing. Duomo still crowded, Uffizi crowded, we walk past Ponte Vecchio — crazy crowded, can barely make it across, then head down the other side of the river where there are much fewer tourists. Walk into random craft shops and galleries where the proprietors are painting or binding books as we come in. A quick buongiorno and we look around while they keep working. Come across a meat shop that also has some wine and olive oil made by the guy working behind the counter! End up on a sleepy winding road walking past houses with gardens and great views of the surrounding hill country. Walked a ton, but saw a beautiful side of Florence that most tourists staying in the town center never see.

That night our group was determined to see the Jersey Shore gang, so we go to their restaurant — we just missed them. Find a spagetteria, eat another great pasta meal, then start walking around and see some TV cameras... turns out it's the Jersey Shore! Well 3 of them anyway: Paulie D, Vinny, Ronny. No Snooki :(

Venice · June 8–10

Venice harbors deep spite for tourists. The Galleria Accademia has Leonardo da Vinci's famous drawing but has never displayed it. Half the museum was under renovation. Road signs require you to examine all signs at an intersection to know where everything is.

But: one very good lunch at Beccafico the second day, after getting a tip from a "local." And a Vivaldi concert that Hannah has already written about.
