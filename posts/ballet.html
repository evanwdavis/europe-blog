Hannah · Vienna · July 3, 2011

Evan and Hannah do “culture”

by Hannah

We couldn't take pictures, but this is the Vienna State Opera House (thanks, google) We were in the lowest level of standing spots (right above the ground level seats), and the view was amazing!

Like my amazing friend, Claire, I am a big fan of classical music.  Despite being mercilessly teased for my tendency to study to Gregorian chants (but really… steady rhythm, soft instrumental accompaniment… perfect background noise),  I have a much more normal set of favorite composers (and a few contemporary soundtrack composers… George Fenton is often on repeat).

So imagine my delight in Venice when I heard about a Vivaldi concert the following night  (touristy rating… off the scale, enjoyable? immensely).  We got our tickets (thanks, Grandpa!!) and 1 hour and two encores later, I was a very happy camper.

Well now we had a problem.  I had the arts bug.  Well, the performing arts bug.  My visual art side was quickly approaching saturation, although this could have been the result of visiting so many museums in Italy.  In Italy, the Catholic church had at least 500 years of being the only institution to commission any artwork, which was strictly controlled with rules on subject matter, colors, style, and composition.  Today, many city museums have decided it is their mission to collect the best religious artwork from thousands of churches in the countryside and display it where larger populations can see it.  Great idea, and I oo-ed and ah-ed at the first twenty Madonna and child depictions. (And they certainly deserved it.  Many pieces are massive, with beautiful gold leaf and impressive realism for the time.)  But after Rome, Florence, Venice, and 2,000 depictions of Madonna and child that were exactly. the. same. even I found it hard to give more than half a second to each one.

Reading about Vienna on the train ride over, I learned that the Vienna State Opera is not only one of the oldest opera houses in the world, but also one of the best (since it has the affiliation of the world famous Vienna Philharmonic orchestra).  Opera… hmm.  Well I had never seen an opera before but certainly, it was on my to do list.  Plus in college I had accidentally signed up for beginner voice lessons with the opera director, an amazing professor who was training the next faces to make the cover of the NY Times Arts & Leisure page.  And then of course I showed up, unable to sing a C, no repertoire to speak of, and with the range of a playskool piano.  I think he took pity on me, as I spent several semesters taking lessons, or maybe I just reminded him of the amazing talent that his other students had.  Either way, I figured seeing an opera could be a small token of appreciation to the man who wasted hours of his life trying to get me to sing above a whisper… and therefore admit I wasn’t good at something (anything sounds good when you… can’t… hear… it…)

So with internet actually available in the room, I eagerly searched for information and tickets to the current opera.   Unfortunately the cheapest tickets were 80 euros a pop.  After seriously considering what the current going rate was for one healthy kidney, I decided it just wasn’t going to happen.  But luckily for us, we were traveling with two good friends, Duncan and Sammi, who were better informed that we were.  They had heard that you can get standing tickets for the opera for much less.

The next morning we eagerly ran to the tourism office to see if we could pick up tickets.  I had my viking hat on and had been warming up “Think of Me” all morning, but unfortunately we were told that tickets could only be purchased 80 minutes before showtime, and we should expect lines to form by the morning for the 300 available tickets since it was opening night.  Well there was no way I was going to spend 8 hours in a line and miss one of our days in Vienna, so we vaguely made plans to stop by in the afternoon but scratched “see an opera” off the list.

After a busy day of site seeing, we ventured back toward the opera house expecting tents, sleeping bags, and picnics to have popped up outside the ticket office.  Surprisingly, all we saw was one elderly man reading a book.  Encouraged, we returned that evening at 5:20pm to take our place in line.  After being repeatedly scrutinized by the fashion police (the opera ushers stationed at the doors who would send people home if not properly attired) we received our 4 euro ticket (I know! Four Euros!!) and were lined up in groups of 20.  At exactly 5:40pm, each group was ushered in to make a run for the best standing spots.

The opera has developed an interesting system for saving standing seats.  Once you have been ushered in, you are allowed to tie a sweater or scarf to the spot in order to save it for the evening.  Even though the show would not start for another hour and a half, all of the standing spaces were full of multicolored scarves within 5 minutes of the doors opening.

The opera itself was an experience, both good and bad.  The music, staging (modern, but appreciable), and talent of the performers was impressive, the story was not.  Written by a Czech composer and set in Russia, it was very typical of the Russian literature genre (full of depression, drinking, suicide, and uncensored sexuality), but expecting the grandeur and costume of Don Giovanni or La Traviata, the story seemed almost dull (in a nutshell: mommy’s boy marries woman, mommy doesn’t approve, woman has affair, throws herself off of bridge after she confesses).  Now it could have more engaging but the lines were exactly the summary I just provided:

Mother: I don’t like your new wife.  You don’t pay attention to me anymore.

Husband: Uhhh…

Wife: I’m so sad.  I’m going to have an affair if you don’t take me with you on your trip.

Husband: Uhhh…

Wife: I’m now in love with random man #1 even though I’ve never talked to him.

Random man #1: I’m in love with you too.

Mother: I don’t like you, wife.

Wife: I’m sorry.  I had an affair.

Mother: You suck.

Wife: Ah, mother-in-law you are so mean. I love you random man #1 but I must go kill myself now.

Random man #: I’m sorry you are going to kill yourself but even though we pledged to always be together 15 minutes ago in a very cliched manner, I don’t really feel like it and am going to go work in St. Petersburg.

Wife: Ok.  Good luck.

(throws herself off bridge)

****The End****

Except it took an hour and a half.  I think I would have been happier not reading the translation.  At least then I could have imagined the witty dialogue and intriguing plot that must be unfolding before my eyes.

Of course the funniest part was how long it took any of us to say a bad word about it.  After an extensive round of applauses and the award of several massive bouquets, the four of us walked out of the opera house quietly.   It was the first opera experience for all of us and no one was going to be the person who admitted they didn’t like it, only to be rebuked by sad, sympathetic faces, pitying the ignorant savage who couldn’t appreciate true art.  So after a few moments of silence, I hesitantly prompted Evan, “So… did you… enjoy your first opera?”  And of course Evan then turned to Duncan, “Well… from my studies of Russian literature I found the Russian theme very… Russian theme-y.”  Duncan: “Ah yes… it was very Russian, though the composer was Czech, which made it… different.  What did you think Sammi?” Sammi: “Well… Russian art is Russian, by nature… but what was your opinion, Hannah?”  And now forced to actually make a potentially polarizing remark, I suddenly yelled, “Look! A unicorn!”  Always works.

In actuality, we all agreed fairly quickly that while we were glad we went, we had no need to ever see Kátja Kabanová again.
